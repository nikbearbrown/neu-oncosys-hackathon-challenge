AI Healthcare Hackathon
at Northeastern
Build practical tools that make oncology data safer, clearer, and more useful for responsible AI.

Track 1 · Cohort Firewall  •  Track 2 · Evidence Navigator

Oncology data holds enormous promise for AI, but only if teams can tell when a dataset is actually fit for the question being asked, and can explain why. The Open Accelerator (TOA), OncoSys AI, and Northeastern University are running a 4-day hackathon, from a virtual kickoff on Wednesday, Nov. 11 to in-person finals in Boston on Saturday, Nov. 14.

The challenge is live. Read the track briefs and the rubric, run the starter, and get your team in by 1:30 PM ET on Thursday. Registration is full, and the waitlist is open on Luma.

The format
4 days, 2 tracks, 1 working prototype
Build a working prototype that helps people use oncology data responsibly. Choose 1 of 2 tracks: check whether data can support a research question, or help people understand the findings and decide what to do next. Bring something that runs to finals. Slides alone won’t do it.

Who
Who should join
Developers, data scientists, designers, researchers, and anyone working where healthcare meets AI. Teams have 3-5 people.

The tracks
One track decides. The other explains.
Both tracks use the same frozen TCGA-BRCA release, real breast cancer data from The Cancer Genome Atlas. Pick the one that fits how you like to work. Your team names its track on the team form, which is due by 1:30 PM ET on Thursday, Nov. 12.

Track 1
Cohort Firewall
Is this dataset ready for this AI question?
Build a tool that checks whether an oncology dataset can support a specific research or analysis question before it is used in an AI workflow. Find data problems, explain why they matter for that question, and show one of 4 results. Show the reasons behind each result so a person can review it.

Blocked
Insufficient Evidence
Suits
Builders who like rules, logic, and data.
What you’ll receive
The frozen TCGA-BRCA data and manifests, with source documentation, checksums, and versioned instructions in the Cancer AI Lab repository.
A research-question card describing the intended use, the group being studied, the outcome of interest, and acceptable limitations.
A simple template for recording checks, findings, confidence, limitations, and data sources.
An unseen test input, such as a changed release or research question, shared during the team’s judging slot to see how its tool handles a new example.
What to submit
A working interface or API that runs against the provided data.
A report showing data problems, such as inconsistent records, missing values, unlikely values, unclear field meanings, bias, information that could give a model an unfair shortcut, and missing source details.
A record linking each result to the checks performed and the data fields used.
A step that asks a person to review the evidence before approving use of the dataset.
Specific steps to address problems in a Blocked or Conditional dataset, with the evidence needed to reconsider the result.
Submit a Git repository with no private information, setup instructions that let judges run your project, a README with a short project summary, and a short demo video. No separate one-page brief is required.

Track 2
Evidence Navigator
Help people understand oncology data and choose a next step
Build an interactive tool that helps people with different levels of technical experience understand what an oncology dataset can and cannot tell them. Make data-quality findings, comparisons, and next steps easy to follow. Clearly explain uncertainty and avoid making conclusions sound stronger than the evidence supports.

Clear evidence
Side-by-side comparisons
Concrete next actions
Suits
People who can make complex findings easy to understand.
What you’ll receive
Summaries and data-quality findings tied to the frozen TCGA-BRCA release used by Track 1, with examples for the featured products.
Example users, such as a data engineer, researcher, data-use reviewer, and program leader.
Records describing each finding, its source, how serious it is, which part of the dataset it affects, its limitations, and a suggested next step.
A short scenario asking the user to decide whether to proceed, fix a problem, narrow the question, or stop.
What to submit
A working interactive experience, not a static presentation.
Views that give each type of user the detail they need while keeping the underlying facts consistent.
A clear way to move from the overall result to the supporting evidence and its sources.
Comparisons between datasets, groups within a dataset, or possible research questions.
Easy-to-understand explanations of uncertainty, missing information, conflicting findings, and data limitations.
A summary recording the chosen next step, the reasons for it, important limitations, and who needs to review it.
Submit a Git repository with no private information, setup instructions that let judges run your project, a README with a short project summary, and a short demo video. No separate one-page brief is required.

How judges will try your prototype
Both tracks
Judges score your submitted prototype, then see how it handles an input you haven’t seen. Depending on the product, the input may be a changed release or question, a new user role, or conflicting findings. At the start of your team’s Q&A, a judge gives you an envelope number and a four-word code. Open your sealed envelope from the challenge repo and run your tool on the file for your product, live. If you pitched your own product, the judges tell you which file to use, if any. Link your required product outputs from your README. Answer keys stay with the organizers.

Get ready this week: pull the latest repo, fetch the TCGA-BRCA data (release A), and rehearse with the practice envelope, using the repo’s envelope guide. Unsealing takes 10 to 20 seconds, so aim for your tool to finish within a minute after that.

Data and safety
Judged on TCGA-BRCA. No PHI. No clinical decisions.
These tools are about whether oncology data can be trusted, so the rules are strict. They apply to both tracks, and they’re worth reading before you start.

You don’t bring the data
Both tracks
Judged work uses the frozen TCGA-BRCA release in the Cancer AI Lab repository on GitHub. It’s real breast cancer data from The Cancer Genome Atlas, where the same patients appear across clinical records, survival, mutations, copy number, RNA, protein, and pathology slides. It’s de-identified but patient-derived, so use it for non-clinical research.

Open the repository and select Run the starter to launch its notebook in Google Colab. In about 15 minutes, it downloads the data, checks each file against the event’s frozen checksums, shows how each join shrinks the cohort, and opens a pathology slide in your browser.

Pitching your own product idea? You may add public, permissively licensed data if you tell organizers at the kickoff and cite it in your README.

Show
What your project shows
Limitations, uncertainty, and data sources.
When a person needs to review a result.
A clean repository. Remove secrets, private links, proprietary datasets, and personal information before judging. Don’t commit raw TCGA files: fetch them with the provided script and verify the pinned checksums.
Credit where it’s due. Follow the upstream licenses, notices, and attribution in the Cancer AI Lab repository.
Never
What stays out
Patient data. No protected health information (PHI), and no identifiable or participant-supplied patient records. Trial-related products use fictional cases only.
Restricted data. No confidential institutional information, production credentials, internal customer records, or controlled-access datasets.
Re-identification. No attempt to re-identify people or infer sensitive patient attributes, and no linking records to outside sources about individuals.
Clinical decisions. No diagnosis, treatment recommendation, or patient risk score.
Overclaiming. No claim of clinical validity, regulatory compliance, or production readiness.
