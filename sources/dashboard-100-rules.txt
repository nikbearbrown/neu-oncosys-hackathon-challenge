Rules for creating information and data visualization dashboards
A good dashboard helps a defined audience understand a situation, make valid comparisons, recognize uncertainty, and decide what to investigate or do next. Its quality depends on the accuracy of those interpretations and decisions.

Below is a comprehensive set of 100 rules, organized so it can serve as a design specification, teaching resource, or review rubric.

Treat numerical integrity, essential accessibility, and permission enforcement as release requirements. Treat choices such as the number of panels, chart density, and whether everything fits on one screen as design decisions to test with users. Research on dashboard design identifies substantial tradeoffs among available space, interaction, audience, and information needs. ieeevis.b-cdn.net
First, identify the dashboard’s purpose
Use these practical categories to establish priorities. A dashboard may combine them, but its main purpose should remain clear.
Dashboard purpose	Main user question	Design emphasis
Operational	What needs attention now?	Current status, exceptions, queues, urgency, ownership, freshness
Management or strategic	Are we moving toward our goals?	Outcomes, trends, targets, meaningful comparisons
Analytical	What patterns deserve investigation?	Distributions, segments, filters, comparisons, detailed evidence
Public or informational	What is happening, and what does it mean?	Plain language, explanation, context, sources, accessible exploration
Personal or workspace	What should I review or do next?	Tasks, deadlines, dependencies, recent changes, useful links


A. Purpose, audience, and scope
1. Name the primary audience. Specify who will use the dashboard, their knowledge, their responsibilities, and the conditions under which they will view it. “Everyone in the organization” is usually too broad to guide design.
2. Write the intended use as a sentence. For example: “Each Monday, program managers identify overdue submissions and determine which fellows need follow-up.” This sentence should guide the content and interaction choices.
3. List the questions the dashboard must answer. Examples include “What changed?”, “Where is performance below target?”, and “Which cases require review?” Prioritize these questions before choosing charts.
4. Give every panel a purpose. A component should support a question, comparison, explanation, or action. Remove panels whose only justification is that the underlying data are available.
5. Match the update frequency to the decision. A monthly planning dashboard may need monthly data. An incident dashboard may need updates within seconds. Faster refresh is useful only when it improves the intended task.
6. Distinguish quick monitoring from extended analysis. A monitoring view should make important conditions immediately noticeable. An analytical view should support careful comparison and exploration. Provide separate views when these needs conflict.
7. Confirm that a dashboard is the appropriate format. A report may explain a complex finding better; a table may support lookup better; an alert may serve an urgent event better. ONS guidance explicitly recommends justifying dashboards through user needs and considering their maintenance and interpretation costs. Office for National Statistics
B. Metric definitions and context
8. Create a metric dictionary before building the interface. For each measure, define its name, formula, unit, population, exclusions, time basis, aggregation method, source, and owner. Terms such as “active,” “completed,” and “successful” require explicit definitions.
9. Treat the denominator as part of the result. “80% completed” is incomplete without explaining completed out of what. Where sample size matters, show both the percentage and the underlying count, such as “48 of 60.”
10. Define what one record represents. A record might represent a person, transaction, event, organization, or daily snapshot. Establish this before joining or aggregating data so the dashboard does not accidentally count the same entity multiple times.
11. Make provenance inspectable. Users should be able to identify the source, responsible producer, and relevant methodology. Label provisional, revised, estimated, or modeled figures accurately. Official statistical dashboard guidance emphasizes source links, status, revisions, and responsible ownership. Office for Statistics Regulation
12. Pair important values with a meaningful comparison. A KPI should usually include a target, previous period, benchmark, or historical range. Name the comparison explicitly; an unexplained upward arrow or “+12%” is insufficient.
13. Define what improvement means. Higher is desirable for some measures, lower for others, and a bounded range for others. Do not automatically color every increase green.
14. Balance activity measures with outcome and quality measures. Submissions, visits, and hours describe activity. Completion, usefulness, reliability, and successful outcomes answer different questions. Include measures that reveal the consequences of increasing volume.
15. Use appropriate numerical precision. Calculate using adequate precision, then format for the task. Avoid decimal places that imply unsupported accuracy. Keep rounding, separators, currency, and abbreviations consistent across cards, charts, tables, and exports.
C. Statistical integrity and trustworthy comparisons
16. Distinguish zero from missing and unavailable data. A measured zero, absent observation, suppressed value, pending report, and failed query mean different things. Give them different labels or states.
17. Aggregate percentages using the correct components. If one group succeeds in 9 of 10 cases and another in 1 of 100, the pooled rate is \(10/110 = 9.1\%\). Averaging 90% and 1% gives 45.5%, which answers a different question.
18. Respect measures that cannot simply be added or averaged. Do not sum overlapping distinct-user counts, add inventory snapshots across time, or average daily percentiles and call the result a monthly percentile. Recompute from suitable underlying data.
19. Make periods and populations comparable. Check eligibility, coverage, units, collection methods, seasonality, and period length. Clearly flag a partial current month when comparing it with a complete previous month.
20. Distinguish percentage points from percentage change. Moving from 20% to 25% is an increase of 5 percentage points and a 25% relative increase. Relative change from a zero baseline is undefined under the usual formula.
21. Inspect what averages and totals conceal. Include distributions, subgroup results, or tail measures when variation affects the decision. An overall improvement may coexist with deterioration for an important subgroup.
22. Show uncertainty when it could change interpretation. Use appropriate intervals or bands and identify what they represent: confidence intervals, prediction intervals, credible intervals, or scenario ranges. Make the boundary between observations and forecasts visible. Office for National Statistics
23. Separate statistical evidence from practical importance. A detectable difference may be too small to matter. An apparent difference may be too uncertain to support a conclusion. Overlapping individual confidence intervals alone do not establish that a difference is statistically insignificant. CDC
24. Keep observations separate from causal explanations. A chart can show an association or a change following an event. Establishing that the event caused the change requires additional evidence. Annotations and summaries should preserve that distinction.
D. Chart selection
25. Choose the relationship before choosing the chart. Identify whether the user needs to compare magnitudes, inspect a trend, understand a distribution, examine a relationship, or locate a geographic pattern. Chart choice should follow that task. Office for National Statistics
26. Prefer aligned positions for precise comparisons. Bars and dot plots on a common scale are strong defaults when users must judge small differences. Graphical-perception research supports common-position encodings over angle and area for the proportional judgments studied. hci.stanford.edu
27. Use tables when exact lookup is the main task. Tables are appropriate for precise values, mixed units, record inspection, and operational lists. A dashboard does not need a chart for every dataset.
28. Use pie and doughnut charts selectively. They can communicate a few distinct parts of one meaningful whole. Avoid them for overlapping categories, separate group rates, incomplete totals, or close comparisons among similarly sized segments. Office for National Statistics
29. Use maps when geography matters to the question. Geographic labels alone do not justify a map. A regional ranking may be clearer as bars. For shaded-area maps, choose an appropriately standardized measure and explain the denominator. Office for National Statistics
30. Require a clear benefit from specialized charts. Consider small multiples before adding more overlapping series. Use gauges, radar charts, treemaps, and Sankey diagrams when their structure helps the intended audience answer the question.
Practical chart-selection guide
User’s task	Useful starting point	Main design check
Compare categories	Horizontal bars or dots	Meaningful order; common scale
Follow change over time	Line chart; columns for interval totals	Correct time spacing; visible gaps
Compare actual performance with a target	Bullet chart or dot/bar with target marker	Target is named and justified
Understand a distribution	Histogram, box plot, or individual points	Sample size, spread, bins, and outliers
Examine two quantitative variables	Scatterplot	Overlapping points; unsupported causal interpretation
Compare parts of a whole	Stacked bars, 100% stacked bars, or a simple pie	Categories form a valid whole
Explain components of a net change	Waterfall chart	Components reconcile to the final total
Compare many groups over time	Small multiples or heatmap	Consistent scales and color meaning
Inspect geographic patterns	Choropleth or proportional-symbol map	Appropriate normalization and geographic coverage
Follow quantities through stages	Sankey diagram or aligned stage comparisons	Flow definitions and totals are valid
Explore hierarchical composition	Treemap or hierarchical table	Users need hierarchy; area comparisons are sufficient
Retrieve exact values or records	Table	Clear headers, units, sorting, and accessible structure


E. Axes, scales, geometry, and ordering
31. Anchor ordinary magnitude bars at zero. Positive and negative bars should extend from a zero baseline. Interval displays, such as Gantt or range charts, encode something different and need appropriately explained endpoints.
32. Allow cropped line or scatterplot axes when justified. A nonzero origin can reveal meaningful variation. Keep bounds and reference values readable so the apparent change remains interpretable. ONS explicitly distinguishes these charts from magnitude bars and filled-area charts. Office for National Statistics
33. Use common scales for direct magnitude comparisons. If neighboring charts use independent scales, make that obvious. Independent scales can support examination of shape, but they change what comparisons are valid.
34. Avoid dual axes as the default. Prefer aligned separate charts or an explicitly indexed comparison when appropriate. Two independently adjustable axes can make unrelated movements appear closely aligned. Office for National Statistics
35. Explain nonlinear scales. Use logarithmic scales for suitable questions, such as comparing multiplicative change. Explain that equal distances represent equal ratios. Do not silently transform, omit, or replace values that the scale cannot represent.
36. Preserve time spacing and missingness. Unequal time intervals should occupy unequal distances on a continuous time axis. Leave visible gaps where regularly expected observations are missing instead of implying uninterrupted measurement. Office for National Statistics
37. Scale area by area. If circles encode quantity through area, their radii must vary with the square root of the values. Doubling a circle’s radius quadruples its area.
38. Avoid visual effects that alter apparent quantities. Decorative 3D, perspective, excessive shadows, and inappropriate smoothing can change how values appear. Do not hide outliers or clip extremes without making the treatment clear.
39. Make ordering and grouping meaningful. Sort by value for ranking tasks, but preserve natural order for time, ages, or process stages. Explain histogram bins, map classes, and other group boundaries; keep them consistent across comparisons.
F. Layout, hierarchy, and typography
40. Put the primary question and most consequential information first. The initial view should establish the dashboard’s subject, scope, and main condition. Allocate prominence according to the user’s priorities.
41. Place evidence that must be compared near each other. Keep a KPI, its benchmark, and its historical trend together. Users should not have to remember a number from another tab to make an important comparison.
42. Allocate space according to the task. A detailed trend may deserve more room than a summary count. Equal-sized cards are appropriate only when they support the information hierarchy.
43. Use a consistent layout system. Align components, standardize spacing, and use clear headings. Let whitespace and grouping communicate relationships. Add borders and containers only when they clarify structure.
44. Preserve a logical reading order across devices. The mobile layout should retain the intended priority and relationships. The programmatic reading order should also make sense to someone using assistive technology.
45. Use progressive disclosure without hiding critical context. Put essential warnings and interpretation details next to the result. Move extended definitions and methodology into accessible supporting layers. OSR recommends this layered approach to communicating quality. Office for Statistics Regulation
46. Treat a single-screen layout as a contextual goal. It can suit monitoring, but scrolling is acceptable when the important information remains prominent. Do not shrink text or remove necessary context to force everything into one viewport. Office for National Statistics
47. Test typography at actual viewing sizes. Use readable labels, predictable heading levels, and adequate line spacing. In tables, align comparable numbers consistently, usually to the right, and consider tabular numerals to aid scanning.
G. Color, labels, and annotation
48. Give color a defined job. Use it to distinguish categories, encode magnitude, indicate deviation, or direct attention. When position and labels already distinguish categories, a single color may be sufficient.
49. Match the palette to the data. Use distinct colors for unordered categories, ordered lightness for increasing magnitude, and a diverging palette around a meaningful midpoint. Avoid implying an order that does not exist.
50. Keep color meanings stable. A category should retain its color across panels, filters, and visits. Reserve status colors for consistent meanings. Check both light and dark themes if both are supported. Office for National Statistics
51. Never make color the sole carrier of important meaning. Pair status colors with text or symbols, and distinguish series with labels, markers, or line styles where needed. This is an explicit WCAG requirement. W3C
52. Avoid rainbow palettes for ordered quantitative values. Uneven perceptual gradients can create apparent boundaries and conceal real changes. Use a palette whose perceived ordering matches the numerical ordering. pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov
53. Write titles that establish the subject and scope. Identify the measure, relevant population, geography, and period through the title and subtitle. If a title states a finding, ensure it updates correctly when the data or filters change.
54. Make units and abbreviations unambiguous. Specify currency, percentage, count, duration, rate denominator, or index base. Explain unfamiliar acronyms. Use time zones where they affect interpretation.
55. Reduce unnecessary label lookup. Prefer direct labels when they remain legible. Keep legends close to their charts and ordered consistently. Use concise annotations for material events, definition changes, revisions, and unusual observations. Office for National Statistics
H. Filters, navigation, and interaction
56. Provide a useful initial view. Users should learn something before configuring controls. Choose sensible defaults and reveal the most important information immediately. Interactivity should serve a concrete user need. Office for National Statistics
57. Make interactive elements recognizable. Users should be able to tell what can be selected, expanded, sorted, or opened. Avoid making unlabeled icons or invisible chart gestures the only route to essential functions.
58. Show which controls affect which panels. Distinguish dashboard-wide filters from local filters. If a comparison or benchmark remains fixed while other data change, label that behavior.
59. Keep the active scope visible. Show selected dates, geography, categories, and other important restrictions. Where useful, show how much data remains, such as “84 of 120 records.”
60. Define filter behavior precisely. Clarify whether selections combine with AND or OR, what “All” means, whether dates include their endpoints, and when changes take effect. Prevent or explain incompatible combinations.
61. Make exploration reversible. Provide clear reset controls and predictable back behavior. Preserve relevant state when users move between overview and detail views.
62. Preserve context during drill-down. Identify the selected subset and its relationship to the whole. A user inspecting one team should still know the period, population, and comparison that led to that view.
63. Keep all representations synchronized. Charts, KPI cards, descriptions, tables, and exports must reflect the same selection. Handle delayed responses so an older query cannot overwrite a newer result.
64. Make shared views reproducible. Preserve relevant filter state in saved views or share links. Distinguish a live view from a frozen snapshot, and a rolling range such as “last 30 days” from fixed dates.
I. Accessibility
For web dashboards, use WCAG 2.2 Level AA as a design and verification baseline. The following rules address particularly common dashboard problems.
65. Make essential functions operable by keyboard. Include filtering, sorting, inspecting values, opening detail views, exporting, and dismissing overlays. Maintain a logical focus order, visible focus, and a clear way out of every component. W3C
66. Meet text-contrast requirements. Ordinary text generally needs at least 4.5:1 contrast; large text needs 3:1. WCAG defines large text as at least 18 points, or 14 points bold, with relevant exceptions and equivalent provisions. W3C
67. Check meaningful graphics and controls separately. Visual information needed to identify controls, their states, or essential graphical content generally needs 3:1 contrast against adjacent colors. This does not mean every decorative gridline needs that contrast. W3C
68. Provide meaningful alternatives to charts. Include an accessible name and a description of important relationships or findings. Offer a properly structured data table when exact values matter. Keep these alternatives synchronized with the selected view. W3C
69. Do not make essential information hover-only. Make equivalent information available through keyboard and touch. Custom hover or focus content generally needs to be dismissible, hoverable, and persistent under the conditions specified by WCAG. W3C
70. Make controls large enough or sufficiently separated. WCAG 2.2 AA specifies 24 × 24 CSS pixels for pointer targets, with spacing and other exceptions. Larger controls are often preferable for frequent touch use. W3C
71. Provide alternatives to dragging. Range selection, panel rearrangement, and similar interactions should also work through suitable buttons, fields, or simple pointer actions. Keyboard support alone does not satisfy the separate requirement for a non-dragging pointer alternative. W3C
72. Support enlargement and reflow. Text should remain usable at 200% enlargement. Support reflow at a width equivalent to 320 CSS pixels; essential two-dimensional content has exceptions, but surrounding controls and explanations still need to adapt. W3C
73. Make status and error messages accessible. Announce results such as “12 records found” or “Export ready” appropriately without taking focus. Describe errors and recovery in text. Avoid announcing every changing data point. W3C
J. Freshness, system states, and performance
74. Separate the data period from the refresh time. “Updated today” does not establish that the underlying observations are current. Show the period represented, relevant source freshness, and last successful refresh; use panel-level dates when sources differ. Office for Statistics Regulation
75. Define when data become stale. Set freshness expectations according to the decision. A missed update should produce a visible stale or incomplete state, including its effect on interpretation.
76. Design every important interface state. Specify loading, empty results, no source data, partial results, failed retrieval, stale data, and denied access. A failed request must never appear as a valid zero or successful “all clear.”
77. Label demonstration data clearly. Keep sample and production data distinguishable. Loading placeholders should look like loading placeholders; they should not display plausible invented figures that users could mistake for measurements.
78. Give users control over automatic updates. Automatically updating information shown alongside other content generally needs pause, stop, hide, or update-frequency control unless essential. Clearly label a paused view. The five-second condition applies to moving content, not to this auto-update requirement. W3C
79. Keep updates visually stable. Avoid moving rows beneath the pointer, changing panel sizes unexpectedly, or repeatedly restarting animations. Respect reduced-motion preferences. Use animation only when it helps users understand a change.
80. Measure performance against real tasks. Track time to useful data and time from filter selection to correct results. For web delivery, Google’s general “good” thresholds are LCP ≤ 2.5 seconds, INP ≤ 200 milliseconds, and CLS ≤ 0.1 at the 75th percentile. These do not replace dashboard-specific measures. web.dev
81. Scale rendering without silently changing meaning. Aggregation, sampling, clustering, and downsampling should preserve the features relevant to the task. Disclose material reductions and provide suitable detail access. Check that performance optimizations do not conceal consequential spikes or rare cases.
K. Alerts and information dashboards
82. Make alerts actionable. An alert should explain the condition, severity, responsible person or team, and next step. Reserve interruptions for conditions that warrant attention; Google’s SRE guidance emphasizes urgency and actionability in alert design. sre.google
83. Distinguish targets from statistical limits. A desired target, a specification limit, a confidence interval, and a process-control limit have different meanings. A process can be stable while failing requirements. Explain what each threshold represents. itl.nist.gov
84. Manage the alert lifecycle. Group duplicate alerts, distinguish acknowledged from resolved conditions, and show persistence or recurrence when relevant. Review alerts that repeatedly produce no useful response.
85. Give informational items enough context for action. A task, incident, approval, or document entry should identify what it is, who owns it, its status, relevant dates, and where supporting evidence can be inspected.
86. Base status and progress on observable conditions. Define what “complete,” “blocked,” “at risk,” and “approved” mean. Display the age of a status when it can become stale. Avoid unsupported percentages of completion.
87. Prioritize information according to the work. Urgency, deadlines, dependencies, and unresolved blockers may matter more than recency. Provide views that help users distinguish new information from work that still needs attention.
L. Security, sharing, and governance
88. Enforce permissions where data are served. Check access for each request, including drill-downs, exports, and saved views. Hiding a control in the interface is insufficient. Apply appropriate permissions to cached and static outputs as well. OWASP Cheat Sheet Series
89. Review disclosure through combinations of values. Sensitive information can become recoverable through totals, small groups, or repeated filtering. Suppressing one cell may be insufficient when its value can be deduced from other cells. Apply appropriate disclosure controls. census.gov
90. Preserve context in exports and print views. Include titles, units, selected scope, date range, source, and relevant caveats. Make clear whether an export contains the displayed subset or a broader dataset.
91. Record consequential changes. Track changes to metric definitions, thresholds, source mappings, permissions, and editable records. Users should be able to understand why a historical value or status changed.
92. Assign ownership for the dashboard’s lifecycle. Name responsibility for data quality, refresh failures, user feedback, accessibility, and maintenance. Review relevance periodically and retire dashboards that no longer meet a useful need. Government Analysis Function
M. Additional rules when AI is involved
These are recommended implementation controls for dashboards that use AI to generate code, interpret data, or produce narrative summaries.
93. Generate numerical claims from verified calculations. Bind summaries to the actual query results, filters, period, and metric definitions. Require a traceable calculation or source for every generated numerical statement.
94. Label the nature of generated content. Distinguish measured facts, model predictions, proposed explanations, and recommendations. A fluent explanation should not silently acquire the status of an observed fact.
95. Review AI-produced dashboards through their behavior and data. Check formulas, joins, denominators, permissions, interaction states, and accessibility. A convincing screenshot cannot establish that the underlying calculations and workflows are correct.
N. Validation and ongoing improvement
96. Prototype with realistic and difficult data. Include long labels, negative values, tiny and large values, missing periods, rare categories, empty selections, and extreme outliers. Neat demonstration data conceal design weaknesses.
97. Reconcile representative results independently. Trace selected values from source records through transformations to the dashboard and export. Check duplicates, join multiplication, time-zone boundaries, zero denominators, and late or corrected records.
98. Test whether users reach correct conclusions. Ask realistic questions: “Which team needs attention?”, “How much did performance change?”, and “What limits this comparison?” Observe their reasoning and errors without coaching them through the interface.
99. Combine automated checks with human accessibility testing. Test keyboard use, screen readers, zoom, touch, and meaningful task completion, ideally with people with disabilities. W3C explicitly states that automated tools alone cannot establish accessibility. W3C
100. Measure usefulness after launch. Review whether users can detect important conditions, interpret evidence correctly, and complete their work efficiently. Use those findings to simplify, revise, or remove components. Page views alone do not demonstrate usefulness.
Example: a well-specified KPI
The following values are illustrative. The example shows how a single metric can remain interpretable without overwhelming the overview.
Element	Example
Metric	Eligible cases resolved by their deadline
Current result	84%, representing 84 of 100 eligible cases
Period	Cases due November 2–8, 2026
Previous comparable period	80%
Change	+4 percentage points, equivalent to a 5% relative increase
Target	90%
Gap to target	6 percentage points below target
Eligibility note	Five training cases excluded; unresolved cases past their deadlines count as unsuccessful
Freshness	Data through November 8, 23:59 ET; refreshed November 9, 08:00 ET
Supporting view	Weekly trend using the same definition
Next investigation	Inspect the 16 late or unresolved cases by team, age, and reason


This example exposes two separate facts: performance improved and performance remains below target. The visual design should allow users to recognize both. A single green arrow would communicate only part of the situation.
Release checklist
Before publishing, require evidence that:
- The primary audience, questions, and intended uses are documented.
- Metric definitions are agreed upon and representative values reconcile to source data.
- Units, denominators, periods, and filter scope are understandable.
- Chart scales and visual encodings support valid comparisons.
- Missing, stale, partial, and failed data cannot be mistaken for valid results.
- Charts, summaries, tables, drill-downs, and exports remain consistent.
- Representative users can complete the main tasks and explain their conclusions.
- Accessibility has been tested through actual interaction.
- Permissions apply throughout the data and export paths.
- Performance is acceptable with realistic data and devices.
- Refresh, correction, maintenance, and retirement responsibilities are assigned.
An incorrect metric, an inaccessible essential task, or unauthorized data exposure should block release regardless of how polished the dashboard looks.
